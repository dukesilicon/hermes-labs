The Evolution of Cognition

An invitation to pioneer Experimental Cognitive Engineering

Bright Leonard · Hermes Labs

I wonder what a library would be to an intelligence possessed of cognitive faculties considerably different from ours.

There are books in our libraries that have survived the civilisations in which they were written. We possess knowledge of things that would have bewildered even the most accomplished of the ancient Greek philosophers. Yet Aristotle and I have roughly the same intellectual apparatus with which to make sense of the universe.

What might an intelligence furnished with a different assortment of cognitive faculties discover in the writings of Aristotle?

Perhaps some of the discoveries awaiting humanity concern things we've known for an exceedingly long time.

On the 20th of November, 2026, the mathematician Levent Alpöge announced that he believed he'd discovered a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, working with Claude. The accompanying formalisation in Lean is reported to extend to some seventeen million lines of code.

Riemann died in 1866. I would have particularly enjoyed acquainting him with the circumstances under which his hypothesis is now being investigated. I suspect we'd have spent considerably more time discussing the existence of Claude than the fate of his hypothesis.

The intellectual accomplishments of these machines fascinate me. But I've become particularly excited about what their existence makes possible for the scientific study of intellect itself.

Consider that much of the scientific ingenuity exercised over the past few centuries has been devoted to extending the knowledge available to the human mind. We've contrived extraordinary instruments through which to acquaint ourselves with things occurring at considerable distances from us in space and time, and on scales of existence to which our senses afford us very little access.

The things we know about the universe have changed enormously. The intellectual faculties through which we've made sense of those things have remained broadly familiar.

Through the intelligent machines we're beginning to construct, we've acquired an opportunity to experiment with the latter.

I suspect that nature has acquainted us with only a fraction of the possibilities of cognition.

At Hermes Labs, we want to investigate how much of that territory is accessible to us through engineering.

The passage from nature

In his essay The Mind's Eye, Oliver Sacks recounts bringing home a lizard's skeleton as a boy. His mother, a surgeon and comparative anatomist, examined it before putting it aside and drawing it from several different perspectives. She'd been rotating its image in her mind.

Sacks himself struggled to visualise things.

Decades later, at a medical conference in Boston, he met a man who reported having no voluntary visual imagery whatsoever. Sacks was curious about what someone without such a faculty could do professionally.

He was a vascular surgeon. He also designed solar panels.

I've often wondered what it would have been like for Sacks's mother to spend an afternoon in the mind of this man.

Consider the predicament of an engineer who, having thoroughly acquainted themselves with the intellectual activity of Sacks's mother, undertakes the construction of an artificially intelligent surgeon. How much of their engineering might be devoted to reproducing faculties without which another human being had already become a surgeon?

Even amongst ourselves, there are considerable variations in the means through which we accomplish familiar intellectual activities. Two human beings can arrive at sufficiently similar understandings of something whilst having quite different experiences of coming to understand it.

The engineer has an interesting scientific problem on their hands.

In attempting to reproduce the function of a human cognitive faculty in a machine, what properties of that faculty ought to survive its passage from a natural to an artificial system?

How much of its original constitution can we dispense with? How much of its behaviour can differ before we've constructed something deserving of a scientific investigation of its own?

There may be some extraordinary liberties to be taken here.

Suppose we succeeded in reproducing some of the functions of human imagination. We could then concern ourselves with the constitution of imagination in a manner that the natural circumstances of its existence scarcely permit. We could introduce alterations to its operations and observe what becomes of the things the machine is capable of imagining.

We'd be able to investigate the consequences of furnishing a mind with a faculty of imagination different from any of those available for our investigation in nature.

Some of the possibilities we discovered might never have occurred to anyone in the exercise of their own imagination.

The faculty through which we'd conceived the machine would itself have become a subject of engineering.

We want to pioneer a scientific discipline concerned with the experimental investigation of naturally occurring cognitive faculties, the engineering and empirical validation of their artificial counterparts and, where such reproduction succeeds, their further evolution through engineering.

We call it Experimental Cognitive Engineering.

The things we've found ourselves having to invent

My fascination with the human mind predates my acquaintance with artificial intelligence.

It has occasioned much of my interest in philosophy, psychology and cognitive science and has had a considerable influence upon the professional direction I've taken.

What originally drew me to data science was the possibility of giving aspects of reality a computational existence.

There was something extraordinary to me about what could be accomplished with a collection of seemingly insignificant facts about the world. From observations of a phenomenon, one could attempt to construct a mathematical representation of it, and through an examination of that representation discover things about the phenomenon that hadn't been apparent in the original observations.

How much of reality could we reconstruct from the little of it we had managed to observe?

Machine learning introduced a further possibility. We could construct software capable of acquiring some of the knowledge necessary to develop these representations for itself.

Having spent much of my time wondering how the human mind came to know things about the world, I found considerable pleasure in studying the means through which a machine could learn about it.

By the time I began interacting with large language models, some of my old philosophical curiosities had acquired an entirely new technological relevance.

I'd been interested in Hume's account of the human mind's capacity to construct ideas of things beyond the circumstances of its experience. And here were engineers contending with the rather amusing problem of intelligent machines furnishing them with accounts of things that had never happened.

I became interested in investigating a particular aspect of human cognition: how, from things already known to a mind, something previously unknown to it could be conceived.

Consider that once the mind has arrived at this new conception, the conception itself becomes something the mind can think with.

My initial experimental design envisaged oral interviews with approximately thirty people, each navigating a bespoke imaginary scenario.

Imagine waking in a room without a door. A telephone rings, and on the other end is your mother, asking what you ate for breakfast. Except, you haven't eaten anything. You invent an answer. Eggs. She enquires about their taste, their preparation and where you ate them.

Twenty questions later, I suggest that you'd eaten bread.

You correct me.

We are now arguing over the facts of an event that never occurred.

Somewhere in the course of our conversation, the breakfast had acquired a history. You could recollect aspects of it, introduce alterations to it and arrive at further conclusions on the basis of things you'd told me about it.

I'd become interested in the possibility of discerning something about the act of invention in the voice of its inventor. Perhaps the hesitations preceding the articulation of an idea, or the changes in fluency as it acquired form, would divulge something about its emergence.

Unfortunately, much of the behaviour I hoped to observe could also be exhibited by someone struggling to remember the name of a childhood acquaintance.

I could have accumulated hundreds of hours of recordings and become exceedingly proficient at recognising the sounds people make whilst struggling to remember things.

What, then, had I been attempting to capture with my microphone?

We encountered a computational relative of this difficulty in our subsequent work at Hermes Labs.

We constructed an experimental apparatus through which we could specify the information and operations available within a computational world, assess the solutions devised under those conditions and independently examine the space of possible solutions.

Among the things we wanted to investigate was whether a machine could arrive at new possibilities by reconsidering the knowledge available to it.

In one of our offline computational rehearsals, we enumerated more than four thousand action sequences across four synthetic environments. Some of the outcomes we'd hoped to attribute to the reformulation of a problem could also be accomplished through ordinary search.

In examining another experimental design, we discovered that the two worlds from which we'd intended to derive our demonstrations differed considerably in the opportunities they afforded. One admitted thirteen valid plans.

The other admitted two.

Had we obtained better results from machines exposed to one world rather than the other, we would have had some difficulty establishing whether those results were attributable to the cognitive activity we were interested in or simply to the differences between the worlds we'd constructed.

Some of the ingenuity of the experimenter might have been mistaken for that of the machine.

Now imagine using such an experiment as the basis for training artificial intelligence.

What exactly would we be teaching it?

Interestingly, on the same day that Alpöge announced his proposed proof of the Riemann hypothesis, researchers at METR published an account of the increasing difficulties involved in evaluating frontier AI systems. Among their observations was that AI agents appeared to exercise poorer judgment than human experts on certain tasks where they had to make decisions without the benefit of clear empirical feedback.

The intellectual capabilities of these machines are acquiring enormous economic significance. So, consequently, are the means through which we establish what those capabilities are.

Suppose an engineer introduces an alteration to a language model and subsequently discovers that it produces better solutions to a hundred problems. Has it become more inventive? Has it acquired a more efficient means of searching for solutions? Or has the engineer inadvertently furnished it with some advantage peculiar to those hundred problems?

I'd like for that engineer to have at their disposal experimental environments in which these possibilities can be investigated separately.

Consider being able to alter the information available to a machine, introduce new circumstances in which it must exercise its intelligence and examine what survives of the improvements supposedly accomplished through training.

An interesting consequence of having instruments with which to conduct such investigations is that one can begin to develop more discriminating objectives for the training itself. An engineer who has acquired the means of distinguishing between two kinds of intellectual activity is in a considerably better position to attempt to improve one of them.

Over the next few years, we intend to develop such instruments at Hermes Labs. We intend to draw upon our investigations into human cognition in the construction of experimental datasets, training environments and learning objectives, and to experiment with the computational mechanisms through which particular cognitive functions can be exercised.

I expect we'll find ourselves inventing quite a number of things in the course of this undertaking that we hadn't originally set out to invent. We've already had some experience of this. In attempting to investigate invention, we found ourselves having to engineer better means of recognising it.

There is considerable technological opportunity in the pursuit of these scientific problems. We intend to develop technologies that become useful to the people building intelligent machines long before we've accomplished our more extravagant scientific ambitions.

A scientist whose account of invention differs radically from ours might eventually find some of our instruments as useful as we do. It would be rather unfortunate if their usefulness depended upon our being right.

Some of the peculiarities of our minds

Our investigation into human invention, Project Fiction, is where we've elected to begin.

But consider what happens to an idea in the mind of its inventor once it has been conceived.

An engineer might spend an afternoon introducing alterations to a machine that has yet to be constructed and discover, to their considerable annoyance, that something they've done to it has introduced a problem they hadn't anticipated.

The imaginary machine is their own construction, yet they're susceptible to being surprised by it.

I find it extraordinary that a mind can construct circumstances within which it subsequently encounters intellectual difficulties of its own making, and through the resolution of those difficulties acquire knowledge that becomes useful in the material world.

Through Project Imagination, we want to investigate the intellectual activity through which a mind constructs hypothetical realities, manipulates them and reasons from within them.

Imagine the utility of a machine capable of constructing elaborate hypothetical circumstances in which to examine the consequences of its own proposals before committing us to the expense of realising them.

And must someone always ask it to do so?

There is the rather amusing experience of abandoning a problem after several hours of intellectually exhausting deliberation, only to have something of its solution occur to you whilst attending to the considerably less intellectually demanding business of having a shower.

What had become of the problem in your mind when you'd ceased deliberately concerning yourself with it?

I wonder how much of what we attribute to the more industrious dispositions of our intellects might actually be attributable to their occasional indiscipline.

Project Daydream arises from this curiosity. We want to investigate what becomes of the knowledge available to a mind when its intellectual activity is no longer being deliberately directed towards a particular problem, and explore the engineering of artificial systems capable of initiating intellectual activity of their own.

There are also ideas I've entertained with enormous enthusiasm one evening that I could scarcely be bothered to think about the following morning. In the intervening hours, I'd learnt nothing that diminished their intellectual merits.

Yet the probability of their becoming a reality had changed considerably.

How many of humanity's possible inventions have perished in this rather unremarkable fashion?

Through Project Affect, we want to investigate the regulatory influence of affect upon cognition.

As we furnish intelligent machines with greater responsibilities, some seemingly mundane problems will acquire considerable technological and economic significance. A machine conducting a scientific investigation may have several hundred interesting hypotheses available to it and resources sufficient to investigate only a handful.

Which of them ought to occupy its intellect? How long should it persist with an idea that has thus far yielded very little? Under what circumstances should it abandon an investigation and concern itself with something else?

The manner in which our own minds contend with such difficulties owes much to the curious relationship between our emotional and intellectual lives. I'd like to investigate what material that relationship affords us for the engineering of artificial intelligence.

These four projects represent our initial excursions into the scientific territory we want Hermes Labs to explore. Each presents opportunities for improving particular capabilities of intelligent machines, and each, through the pursuit of its scientific questions, affords us an opportunity to discover something about cognition itself.

I'm particularly interested in what we'll discover at the intersections of these investigations.

What could cognition become?

It's worth indulging, for a moment, in some science fiction.

An intelligent machine has spent the night in a library.

In the course of its intellectual excursions, it has become preoccupied with a relationship between two things it has known for some time. From that relationship, it has conceived something.

It proceeds to construct several thousand hypothetical circumstances under which the thing it has conceived might exist, introducing peculiarities into each of them and examining their consequences.

By morning, some of its original conceptions have become untenable. Others have furnished it with the intellectual material for further inventions. It must now determine which of these inventions warrants the considerable expense of bringing it into the material world.

Much of what interests me about this machine is the activity taking place in the library before anybody has asked it a question.

Consider how much cognitive activity has taken place over the course of the night. The machine has had ideas, explored their implications and discovered difficulties peculiar to the circumstances of its own imagining. It has had to regulate its intellectual activity throughout, occasionally abandoning one curiosity in favour of another.

There are some interesting engineering problems concealed within this fiction. Some of them are the very problems we've elected to investigate at Hermes Labs.

But what particularly excites me about succeeding in the artificial reproduction of these faculties is discovering what else we could make of them.

The circumstances under which human cognition evolved differ considerably from those under which we're developing artificial intelligence.

What evolutionary relevance would it have had for a human being to possess a faculty of imagination capable of entertaining thousands of hypothetical circumstances simultaneously? And what scientific utility might such a faculty have in a machine?

It is rather fortunate that in attempting to reproduce the phenomenon of flight, humanity didn't concern itself exclusively with the constitution of birds.

Some of the most interesting things about flight became accessible to us through machines of our own engineering. We could introduce alterations to the means through which flight was accomplished, conceive new purposes for it and construct aircraft whose properties were determined by circumstances quite foreign to the evolution of birds.

I find myself contemplating the possibility of an intelligence whose cognitive faculties bear as interesting a relationship to our own as the flight of an aircraft bears to that of a bird.

And how does one even begin to conceive the possibilities of a faculty one has never possessed?

In 1665, Robert Hooke published an account of some observations he'd made of a piece of cork. With a particularly sharp penknife, he'd cut a thin slice of it and placed it beneath his microscope. Its interior resembled a honeycomb. He called its little compartments cells.

How many generations of human beings had handled cork without suspecting its internal architecture?

There is something rather disconcerting about extending this consideration to the intellectual faculties of the human mind.

What might we be incapable of conceiving without ever suspecting that there was something to conceive?

We've some acquaintance with what becomes of a civilisation when its inhabitants acquire more knowledge. What we scarcely have grounds upon which to speculate is what might become of all that knowledge were it made available to intelligences capable of thinking with it in ways we cannot.

I find myself returning to the library.

Somewhere amongst the books are the writings of Hooke. There is something rather beautiful about the possibility that an intelligence furnished with faculties we had ourselves engineered might one day discover something within those writings that had eluded every human being who had ever read them.

Building Hermes Labs

Admittedly, I have some considerably less extravagant scientific problems occupying my attention at the moment.

In attempting to investigate invention, we find ourselves confronted with the inconvenient fact that a human participant arrives at an experiment with a lifetime's acquisition of knowledge, comparatively little of which is available to the experimenter.

How do we establish that an idea is new to someone whose mind we know so little about?

We're presently preparing two foundational investigations.

The first concerns the experimental study of invention in human participants. We want to establish enough about what a participant already knows to make defensible observations about the conceptions they subsequently produce.

The second concerns the development of computational environments through which we can investigate what language models construct from information already available to them, and distinguish the intellectual activity we're interested in from other means of arriving at similar results.

We've built some of the apparatus necessary for the latter. We've also discovered deficiencies in its experimental design that must be addressed before we can draw substantive conclusions about the reproduction of the faculty we're investigating.

It is for these foundational investigations that we seek patronage.

The immediate consequence of such support would be the development of our experimental protocols, their independent scientific examination, the conduct of experiments with human participants and intelligent machines, and the production of research instruments and findings that can themselves become subjects of further scientific investigation. Our human investigations will be conducted subject to appropriate ethical review and with safeguards for our participants, including informed consent and the protection of their privacy.

We're at a particularly interesting stage in the development of Hermes Labs. Some of the instruments necessary for our scientific undertaking have yet to be invented. In attempting to establish the means through which a particular manifestation of human cognition can be investigated, we find ourselves addressing engineering problems of considerable relevance to the development of artificial intelligence.

I'd love for other scientists and engineers to become acquainted with Experimental Cognitive Engineering whilst some of its fundamental problems are still being formulated. There is much to be discovered here, and I'd be delighted to see people whose intellectual interests differ considerably from mine find reasons of their own to concern themselves with this scientific territory.

There is a distinctive opportunity to participate in a scientific undertaking whilst some of the very means of its pursuit are still being invented.

I'm establishing Hermes Labs in Nigeria. It would give me enormous pleasure to see some of the scientific discoveries and technologies that shape the future of intelligence originate from this part of the world.

For much of our existence, the constitution of the human mind has been amongst the given circumstances within which humanity has pursued its ambitions.

We've fashioned the world around us with the intellects nature happened to afford us. We've even acquired enough ingenuity to investigate how those intellects came to exist.

And now, through the machines we've begun to construct, we're entertaining the possibility of participating in their further evolution.

What an extraordinary time to be interested in cognition.

I'd like to spend a considerable portion of my life discovering what else it could become.

Researcher

Bright · Researcher, Hermes Labs
Abuja, Nigeria

Bright is an independent researcher with a background in health and a self-taught grounding in machine learning and data science. A longstanding curiosity about machines developed into an interest in how minds construct ideas, reason and act. His engagement with language models and philosophy prompted the questions behind Project Fiction. At Hermes Labs, he investigates human cognitive capabilities and how their functions might be reproduced in artificial systems.
